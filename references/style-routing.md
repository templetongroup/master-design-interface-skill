# Style routing and creative direction

Use this reference when the user requests a new direction, supplies visual references, or asks to make an interface distinctive without losing coherence.

## Separate invariant quality from aesthetic choice

Every direction must preserve clarity, accessibility, robust layout, honest state, platform behavior, and system consistency. Style controls the expression of those qualities, not whether they apply.

Choose a dominant character from the product context:

| Character | Works well for | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Precise utilitarian | Professional tools, operations, dense workflows | Becoming generic enterprise UI or visually joyless |
| Refined hardware | Native tools, audio, creative utilities, instrumentation | Decorative screws, glare, fake texture, or controls that imitate hardware at the cost of clarity |
| Warm editorial | Knowledge, culture, narrative, considered brands | Repeated hanging labels, oversized serif theater, and weak control affordances |
| Minimal product | Focused consumer tools and quiet premium products | Empty space without hierarchy, faint contrast, undifferentiated cards |
| Industrial/brutalist | Cultural, experimental, or strongly opinionated brands | Novelty that harms scanability, trust, or platform expectations |
| Playful expressive | Learning, community, youth, creativity | Excess motion, toy-like controls, and uneven density |
| Luxury restrained | High-consideration services and premium goods | Thin low-contrast type, vague copy, and ornamental delay |

Use these as starting points, not templates. A product can combine a **visual foundation** with a different **workflow model** when the pairing is explicit—for example, refined hardware surfaces with guided workbench flows.

## Direction format

When the choice is open, present 2–3 directions using the same headings:

- premise and product fit;
- macrostructure and density;
- typography roles;
- palette, material, and imagery;
- component and interaction character;
- one or two signature moves;
- risks and what the direction deliberately avoids.

Recommend one. Do not present three nearly identical palettes or make the user reverse-engineer the tradeoff.

## Reference handling

Classify each visual input:

- **Acceptance target**: match measured structure and component relationships closely.
- **Brand source**: use the approved mark, palette, type, and voice while designing the interface independently.
- **Content source**: preserve information, not visual style.
- **Inspiration**: extract principles and design DNA; do not replicate distinctive composition or content.

Measure before interpreting: viewport, content bounds, columns, gaps, control heights, radii, type sizes/weights, color roles, image ratios, density, and fold. State which values are measured and which are inferred.

Recreate the hierarchy and relationships first. Do not paste surface motifs onto the old layout, and do not reproduce an accidental flaw merely because it appears in a screenshot.

## Anti-slop without aesthetic dogma

Reject common generated defaults when they are unmotivated:

- purple-blue glow gradients and neon halos;
- three identical feature cards regardless of content;
- every element in a rounded floating container;
- a filled primary plus ghost secondary repeated mechanically;
- arbitrary pills, oversized radii, fake glass, heavy generic shadows;
- all-centered composition and uniform section rhythm;
- placeholder copy, fake people, round-number metrics, and decorative AI jargon;
- random icon families or emoji standing in for interface icons;
- giant headlines unsupported by content or product tone;
- decoration used to compensate for weak hierarchy.

These are not absolute bans. A gradient, equal grid, pill, glass surface, serif headline, or centered layout is valid when the brand, content, platform, and surrounding system make it intentional. State the reason when using a pattern that is frequently generic.

## Brand coherence

Treat an approved brand kit as a source of truth. Map brand assets into product roles rather than sprinkling the logo everywhere:

- mark and wordmark usage;
- interface and display type;
- semantic and decorative colors;
- imagery direction;
- voice and naming;
- geometry and material cues.

Protect clear space, aspect ratio, legibility, and one-color/reversed use. Confirm supplied assets render at real interface sizes. Brand presence should make the product recognizable even when the logo is absent.

## Distinctiveness test

At the end of direction work, answer:

1. What would remain recognizably this product in grayscale?
2. What is the one composition or interaction move competitors are unlikely to share?
3. Which parts are deliberately ordinary because convention helps users?
4. Can the system support every real screen, or only a hero mockup?

If distinctiveness depends only on accent color, the direction is not developed enough.
